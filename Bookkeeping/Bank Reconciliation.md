# How To Conduct A Bank Reconciliation 

On a high level a Bank Reconciliation is a detailed assessment of comparing the both your bank statement balance and GL balance to determine how different the balances are. 

There's typically more than one reason of why a bank statement reason and GL don't match which are as follows

1. Omissions: Omissions occur when transactions appear on the bank statement but have not yet been recorded by the business in its Cash Book.
Often, a business remains unaware of these transactions until they receive and review their monthly statement. Common omissions include:

- Missing Receipts: Electronic fund transfers or deposits made directly into the account that the accountant has not yet entered
- Interest Received: Interest earned on the cash balance in the bank account
- Bank Fees: Service charges or maintenance fees deducted by the bank
- Bounced Cheques (NSF): Checks from customers that were deposited but later rejected by the bank because the customer had "Not Sufficient Funds"

2. Timing Differences: Timing differences happen when transactions are recorded in the Bank Statement and the Cash Book during different accounting periods. The two most common examples are:
- Deposits in Transit: Cash or checks that the business has received and recorded in its books at the end of the month, but which the bank has not yet processed or shown on the statement
- Outstanding Cheques: Checks written and sent to suppliers by the business (and recorded in the Cash Book) that have not yet been cashed by the recipient.

3. Errors: Differences also arise when someone "messes up" the recording of a transaction. While bank errors are possible, the sources note that errors are more frequently made by the business's accountant or bookkeeper. Examples include:
- Transposition/Typo Errors: For instance, a bookkeeper might incorrectly enter a check for $7,050 into the General Ledger as $7,005.
- Bank Errors: Though less common, the bank may occasionally record an incorrect amount or transaction, which must be identified so the bank can fix it.

The purpose of a bank reconciliation is to identify every one of these differences to calculate the "True Cash Balance" and ensure the business's books provide an accurate financial picture.
